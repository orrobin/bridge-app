title: Attachment Styles: A Rough Map, Not a Diagnosis
author: Bridge Team (original explainer)
evidence_strength: informed-by-published-research
---
Attachment theory describes broad, tendencies-level patterns in how people approach closeness,
conflict, and reassurance in relationships, originally developed from research on caregiver-child
bonds and later extended to adult romantic relationships. Four broad patterns are commonly
described: secure (comfortable with closeness and independence), anxious (craves closeness,
sensitive to signs of distance), avoidant (values independence, uncomfortable with too much
closeness too fast), and disorganized/fearful-avoidant (wants closeness but expects it to go
wrong).

These are tendencies that can shift depending on context and the specific relationship, not fixed
personality types, and definitely not something reliably diagnosable from a handful of text
messages with a new match. A person texting slowly might be avoidant, busy, anxious about
seeming too eager, or simply not a frequent texter -- attachment style is one hypothesis among
several, not a conclusion.

Where this framework is genuinely useful is turned inward: noticing one's own recurring patterns
-- do I spiral when a reply is slow? do I pull back the moment things feel close? -- tends to be
more actionable than trying to diagnose someone else's attachment style from limited
information.
